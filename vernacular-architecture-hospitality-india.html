How Vernacular Architecture Creates Distinctive Hospitality Destinations
Regional Traditions for Modern Hotels

Ar. Udit Vishnoi 24 November 2026 12 min read

A guest who wakes up in a hotel in Kerala, Kumaon or Rajasthan wants to know where they are. Vernacular architecture is how a hotel answers that question with its walls, roofs, courtyards and craft.

Too many Indian hotels look the same from Shimla to Kochi: a concrete frame, a glass lobby and a lawn. They may be comfortable, but they give guests no reason to remember them and no reason to come back. A hotel that draws on the building traditions of its region offers something a generic box cannot. It feels rooted and often performs better in the local climate. It also gives work to the craftspeople who keep those traditions alive.

This guide explains how vernacular architecture creates distinctive hospitality destinations in India. It covers what vernacular means, the regional traditions hotels draw on, how to adapt them for modern use, structure and safety, working with craftspeople, cost and the mistakes that turn tradition into theme-park décor. It sits alongside our guides on climate-responsive hotel design and eco lodge architecture, which go deeper into climate and low-impact building.

What Vernacular Architecture Means

Vernacular architecture is the way ordinary people in a place have built for generations, usually without architects. It grows out of four things: the climate, the materials at hand, the skills of local builders and the way people live. A Kumaoni house with a stone ground floor and timber upper floor, a Kerala house with a deep sloping roof and a central courtyard, and a Rajasthani haveli with thick walls and carved screens are all answers to those four conditions.

In hospitality, vernacular design means learning from those answers. It is not about copying an old building exactly. A hotel needs lifts, fire exits, air conditioning, accessible rooms and commercial kitchens that no village house ever had. The skill is in keeping what made the tradition work, such as its form, its materials, its relationship to the land and its craft, while meeting the needs of a modern hotel.

Vernacular, heritage and theme

  • Heritage is an existing old building, restored or converted. Our guide to heritage hotel adaptive reuse covers that work.
  • Vernacular is new building that learns from local tradition.
  • Theme is decoration applied to an ordinary building to suggest tradition. It is the version guests see through quickly.

Why It Matters for Hotels and Resorts

  • A reason to choose you. In a market full of similar hotels, a strong sense of place is one of the few things competitors cannot copy easily.
  • Comfort with less energy. Traditional forms evolved to handle local heat, rain and cold. Courtyards, verandahs, thick walls and sloping roofs reduce the load on machines.
  • A fit with the landscape. Buildings that use local stone, timber and roof forms sit quietly in hill, forest and rural settings, which matters to guests and often to approving authorities.
  • Local employment and craft. Masons, carpenters, stone carvers and weavers find work during construction and often afterwards in maintenance and interiors.
  • Stories to tell. Every material and detail has a source, which gives the hotel content for guests, guides and marketing.

The right tradition is the one that belongs to the site. A Rajasthani haveli in the Himalayas is as out of place as a glass tower.

How to Adapt Tradition for a Modern Hotel

We work on five levels, from the scale of the whole site down to the smallest detail.

1. Site planning

Traditional settlements follow the land. Hill villages step along contours, desert towns cluster around water and shade, and riverside towns face the ghats. A resort can learn the same pattern. At Taj Rishikesh, the project description explains that the site planning follows a traditional Himalayan village anchored around a Darbargadh, with villas placed on the natural terraces along the contours to reduce cutting and filling.

2. Form and roof

The roof is often the strongest regional signal. A deep, steep tiled roof says Kerala. A slate roof with wide eaves says the western Himalaya. Getting the pitch, overhang and proportions right does more for a sense of place than any amount of ornament.

3. Materials

Local stone, timber, brick, lime and earth tie the building to the ground it stands on. Where a traditional material is no longer available or allowed, find an honest substitute that weathers in a similar way.

4. Spatial sequence

Many Indian traditions organise life around courtyards, verandahs and thresholds that move from public to private. A hotel can use the same sequence: a gateway, a forecourt, a shaded verandah and then the inner courtyard.

5. Craft and detail

Carved timber, stone screens, inlay, textiles and metalwork bring the tradition indoors. Craft works best where guests can touch it: doors, railings, headboards, ceilings and furniture.

Guest Rooms and Public Spaces

Vernacular character has to carry through to the places where guests spend their time.

Arrival and public areas

The arrival is where the tradition should be strongest. A gateway, a forecourt and a shaded verandah or courtyard before the lobby make guests slow down and notice where they are. Public spaces can carry the most ambitious craft, such as a carved ceiling, a stone screen or a courtyard with water, because everyone passes through them. Our guide to hotel lobby and public area design covers the arrival sequence in more detail.

Guest rooms

Rooms need modern comfort first: a good bed, a quiet air-conditioning system, strong showers and reliable lighting. Within that, a few vernacular elements make a room feel local without making it a museum:

  • A verandah, balcony or window seat that connects the room to the outdoors the way traditional homes do.
  • Local materials at touch points, such as a stone floor, a timber ceiling, a carved headboard or lime-plastered walls.
  • Regional textiles in curtains, cushions and throws, which are easy to refresh.
  • Proportions and openings that follow the tradition, such as small deep windows in cold regions or tall shuttered openings in hot, humid ones.

Keep the bathroom contemporary and easy to clean. Guests enjoy a carved door far more than a traditional plumbing fitting.

Structure, Safety and Building Codes

Traditional construction carries lessons and limits. Kath-kuni, the interlocking timber-and-stone system of Himachal, is a good example. A 2021 study of masonry structures in Himachal Pradesh by Sharma, Kumar and Sarhosis found that the symmetrical form of kath-kuni buildings makes them less vulnerable to earthquakes. It also found that mass concentrated at the top weakens the upper portion against lateral forces, and that some structures had deteriorated through a lack of regular maintenance.

For a hotel that will hold paying guests, these lessons have to be combined with modern engineering. In practice there are three ways to build:

  • True load-bearing construction. Stone, brick or earth walls carry the building. It suits small, low-rise projects and needs an engineer who understands the system.
  • Hybrid construction. A concrete or steel frame carries the loads, and local materials form the walls, cladding, roofs and finishes. This is the most common approach for larger hotels.
  • Frame with vernacular finishes only. The quickest route, and the one most likely to slide into theme if done carelessly.

The Sunyata Eco Hotel in Chikmagalur shows how far load-bearing construction can go. According to STIR, Design Kacheri designed the 10,000 sq ft hotel largely as a load-bearing structure to reduce concrete, using rammed earth, mud blocks, filler slabs, jack arch roofs, vaults and precast clay roofs, around a courtyard in the thotti mane style.

Whatever the system, the building must meet the structural, seismic and fire requirements that apply to hotels. Timber and thatch need fire treatment and careful detailing near kitchens and electrical equipment. Hill sites need engineering for their seismic zone. Our guide to mountain hotel architecture covers hill building rules in more detail.

Working With Local Craftspeople

Vernacular design depends on people as much as materials. Bring the right craftspeople in early, or the result will look like a factory imitation.

  • Find them during design. Visit workshops and quarries, see what local masons and carpenters can do and design around their skills.
  • Make samples and mock-ups. A full-size mock-up of a wall, a door or a screen tests the finish, the cost and the time before the whole building is committed.
  • Allow for time. Hand-carved stone and timber take longer than factory products. Order early and plan the programme around it.
  • Document the details. Drawings that record joints, profiles and patterns help when work is repeated across many rooms and when repairs are needed later.
  • Mix craft with standard work. Use hand craft where guests see and touch it, and standard construction for back-of-house and services.

Our interiors for Sangam Pooja Wellness Resort in Prayagraj follow this approach. The palette of marble with inlay, carved wood, brass and lime plaster carries Indo-Islamic craft through the vaulted lobby, the arched dining room and the suites. See the Sangam Pooja interiors.

How We Research a Region Before Designing

Vernacular design starts with looking closely. Before drawing a hotel, we spend time understanding how the region builds.

  1. Walk the area. Visit villages, old houses, temples and markets around the site, and photograph roofs, walls, doors, windows and how buildings meet the ground.
  2. Record what works. Make measured sketches of a few good buildings, noting wall thickness, roof pitch, overhangs, room sizes and how air and light move through them.
  3. Talk to builders. Local masons and carpenters know which stone weathers well, which timber lasts and which details fail.
  4. Find the materials. Locate quarries, timber suppliers, brick kilns and lime works, and check supply, quality and any legal restrictions on use.
  5. Check the rules. Hill states, heritage zones and eco-sensitive areas often have their own guidance on height, roofs and materials.
  6. Choose what to carry forward. Decide which elements will shape the plan, the form, the materials and the craft, and which belong only in the past.

Climate Lessons From Tradition

Traditional buildings were the original climate-responsive architecture. Most of their features still work:

  • Courtyards draw light and air into deep plans and let warm air escape.
  • Verandahs and deep eaves keep sun and rain off the walls and give comfortable outdoor space.
  • Thick walls of stone or earth slow down heat gain in hot climates and hold warmth in cold ones.
  • Jaalis and screens filter light, give privacy and let air through.
  • Steep roofs shed heavy monsoon rain and snow.
  • Water in courtyards and stepwells cools the air through evaporation.

Using these features cuts energy use and makes the hotel more comfortable in the shoulder seasons, when guests would rather open the windows.

Cost and Time

Vernacular design is not automatically cheaper or more expensive. Local stone and timber can save transport costs, and passive cooling saves on running costs for the life of the hotel. Hand craft, mock-ups and slower construction add cost and time. Skilled artisans may be scarce in some regions, and their availability can set the pace of work.

The most reliable approach is to decide early where craft adds the most value, usually in the arrival, public areas, facades and guest rooms, and to budget for it from the start. For overall cost benchmarks per key, see our guide on how to design a resort in India.

Mistakes That Turn Tradition Into Theme

  • Borrowing from the wrong region. Rajasthani arches in Kerala or Kerala roofs in Rajasthan.
  • Ornament on a generic box. Carved panels glued to a standard concrete block fool nobody.
  • Imitation materials. Printed stone tiles and plastic timber age badly and look cheap up close.
  • Ignoring maintenance. Timber, lime and thatch need regular care. Plan the budget and the team for it.
  • Services that break the illusion. Air-conditioning units, pipes and cable trays on a carved facade undo the whole effort. Plan their routes early.
  • Skipping the engineer. Traditional systems need engineering for modern loads, seismic zones and fire safety.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is vernacular architecture in hospitality?

It is hotel and resort design that learns from the traditional building of its region, including site planning, roof forms, materials, courtyards and craft, while meeting the needs of a modern hotel such as lifts, fire safety, air conditioning and accessible rooms.

Is vernacular architecture more expensive for a hotel?

Not necessarily. Local materials can reduce transport costs and passive cooling lowers running costs, but hand craft, mock-ups and slower construction add cost and time. Deciding early where craft adds the most value keeps the budget under control.

Are traditional building systems like kath-kuni safe for hotels?

Research on kath-kuni buildings in Himachal Pradesh found that their symmetrical form makes them less vulnerable to earthquakes, but that mass concentrated at the top and poor maintenance are weaknesses. For a hotel, traditional systems should be engineered to modern structural, seismic and fire requirements, often as a hybrid with a modern frame.

How do you avoid a themed look?

Borrow from the tradition of the site's own region, use real materials, get the roof form and proportions right before adding ornament, put craft where guests touch it and plan services so they do not appear on the facade.

Which regions in India suit vernacular hotel design?

Almost every region has a tradition to draw on. Common sources include the timber-and-stone buildings of Himachal and Uttarakhand, the havelis of Rajasthan and Gujarat, Kerala's courtyard houses, Karnataka's courtyard homes, Goa's Indo-Portuguese houses and the Indo-Islamic architecture of Uttar Pradesh.

Build a Hotel That Belongs Somewhere

The strongest hotels in India feel like they could only exist where they are. That comes from reading the region's own way of building and adapting it honestly, with real materials, real craft and modern engineering underneath.
