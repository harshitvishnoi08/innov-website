Hotel Master Planning in India
How a Hotel Site Is Planned, Step by Step

Ar. Udit Vishnoi 19 November 2026 12 min read

Most hotel projects that go wrong were already in trouble before the first drawing of a guest room. The site was misread, the programme was a guess, or the land was laid out so that the hotel could never grow. A master plan is where those problems are caught.

When a landowner or hotelier comes to our studio, the first question is usually about the building: how many floors, what style, how many rooms. We answer those questions, but only after a stage most people skip, the hotel master plan. It decides what goes on the land, how big each part is, how guests and staff move, and how the property can grow over the next ten or twenty years.

This guide explains what hotel master planning services actually involve, stage by stage, and how the process works in India. It draws on master plans our team has prepared for hospitality sites such as a riverfront wellness resort in Prayagraj and an 11-acre hill resort community in Mukteshwar.

What a Hotel Master Plan Is (and What It Is Not)

A hotel master plan is a long-term plan for the whole site. It fixes the position and size of every major part of the property: the arrival, the main building or buildings, rooms, restaurants, banquet and meeting space, leisure, parking and service areas. It also sets out what gets built first and what is left for later.

It is not the detailed building design. Floor plans, elevations, room layouts and interiors come afterwards and work within the master plan. Think of the master plan as the decisions that are expensive to change once concrete is poured, and the building design as the detail that fills them in.

Master plan decides Building design decides
Where the hotel sits on the land and how guests arrive The look of the facade and the arrival lobby
How many rooms, and in what mix of types The layout and size of each room and bathroom
How much area goes to restaurants, banquet, spa and back-of-house How each of those spaces is laid out and finished
Separate routes for guests, service and staff Corridors, lifts and staircases inside the building
What is built in phase one and what is reserved for later The construction drawings for each phase

Stage 1: Reading the Site

Every master plan starts with a site analysis. Before we place anything, we study the land and its surroundings and write down what it offers and what it rules out. For a hotel, the checks that matter most are:

  • Visibility and approach. A city or highway hotel lives on being seen and easy to reach. Where can drivers see it from, and where can they turn in safely?
  • Views and orientation. Rooms and restaurants should face the best outlook, whether that is a river, a hill or a quiet garden, and avoid the harsh west sun.
  • Slope and soil. On a hill, the gradient decides where buildings can sit without heavy cutting and retaining. At Mukteshwar, a slope analysis of the ridge set where every villa could go.
  • Access for service. Trucks, waste and staff need their own entry that guests never see. If the site only has one usable road edge, this has to be solved first.
  • Rules that apply to the land. Land use, road widths, setbacks, height limits and any hill, river or eco-sensitive restrictions. These can change the whole plan.

At the Sangam Pooja resort in Prayagraj, the riverbank analysis came before anything else. The 2.5-acre master plan places the main palace building at the arrival and steps the resort villas down toward the water along the natural gradient, so most of the site shares the river view.

Stage 2: The Space Programme

The space programme is the list of everything the hotel will contain and roughly how big each part is. It turns a business idea into areas that can be planned. A weak programme is the most common reason a master plan has to be redone, so we build it with the owner and, where there is one, the operator or hospitality consultant.

Room count and mix

The programme starts with the number of rooms (keys) and the mix of standard rooms, larger rooms and suites. The mix matters as much as the total, because suites and larger rooms change the building's width and structure. For typical room sizes by category, see our standard hotel room size guide.

Food, banquet and meeting space

In India, banquets and weddings often bring in as much as the rooms do, so they deserve careful sizing. A business or convention hotel needs meeting rooms close to the lobby. The Archgyan hotel design guide gives a rule of thumb of about 6–8 m² of meeting space per guest room for a convention hotel.

Back-of-house

Kitchens, stores, laundry, staff areas, engineering and offices are what make the hotel work. The same guide puts back-of-house at around 15–25% of gross floor area for a full-service hotel. Under-sizing it is one of the most common and costly mistakes we see.

Parking

Parking takes up a large share of most hotel sites, especially where banquets draw hundreds of cars. As a rough starting point, codes commonly require about 0.8–1.2 parking spaces per guest room, but the local bye-laws decide the real figure, and banquet halls usually add their own requirement on top.

These ratios are international rules of thumb, useful at the start of a project. The final programme should be checked against the local building rules and, for a branded hotel, the operator's own standards.

How the programme changes with the type of hotel

The same list of spaces carries very different weight depending on what the hotel sells. Three types we see often in North India:

Hotel type What drives the programme What usually gets under-planned
City business hotel Room count, an all-day restaurant, meeting rooms and a compact lobby on a small, costly plot Parking and the service entry, which compete for the same narrow road frontage
Highway or banquet-led hotel Banquet halls, lawns and kitchens sized for weddings, with rooms as a smaller share Parking for event days and kitchen capacity for several functions at once
Resort-style hotel Low-rise rooms or villas spread across the land, with pool, spa and gardens as the main attraction Staff housing, stores and the distances housekeeping and room service have to cover

Questions to settle before the programme is fixed

  • Who is the guest? Business travellers, wedding parties, weekend families and pilgrims all need different room mixes and public spaces.
  • Will a brand operate it? A branded hotel comes with its own standards for room sizes, back-of-house and amenities, and it is far cheaper to plan for them from day one.
  • Where does the revenue come from? If banquets earn as much as rooms, the banquet kitchen, lawn and parking deserve the same attention as the guest floors.
  • What must open first? Knowing the first phase early lets the programme be split so that phase one works on its own.

Stage 3: Zoning the Site

With the programme in hand, the master plan places each part on the land. Almost every hotel organises into the same zones: arrival, public areas (lobby, restaurants, banquet), rooms, leisure (pool, spa, gym) and service. What separates a good plan from a poor one is how those zones sit next to each other.

  • Arrival and public areas close together, so banquet and restaurant guests never pass through the rooms.
  • Rooms in the quietest part of the site, with the best views and away from the banquet lawn's late-night music.
  • Service on its own route, from the gate to the kitchens and stores, without crossing guest paths.
  • Leisure as a link between rooms and public areas, so the pool and gardens become part of the view from both.

We go deeper into the five zones and how guests move between them in our guide to designing a resort in India, which applies just as well to a resort-style hotel.

Stage 4: How Many Rooms Can the Land Take?

Owners ask this more than anything else. The answer comes from two directions: what the rules allow, and what the market and guest experience need.

What the rules allow

Indian development rules control how much can be built on a plot through a few key limits:

  • FAR (floor area ratio), also called FSI. The total built-up area allowed, as a multiple of the plot area. A plot of 1 acre with an FAR of 1.5 can carry about 1.5 acres of floor area across all floors.
  • Ground coverage. The share of the plot the building can cover at ground level.
  • Height limit. Set by local rules, road width, airport or heritage zones, and hill-development regulations.
  • Setbacks and parking. Open margins around the building and the number of parking spaces, which both take land away from building.

Hotel FAR and coverage vary widely between cities and between master plan zones, so they must be confirmed for your exact plot with the local authority before any room count is promised.

What the experience needs

The rules set a ceiling. How close you build to it depends on the kind of hotel. A city business hotel usually builds close to its allowed area and stacks rooms over a podium of public spaces. A resort-style hotel does the opposite and spreads across the land. In our resort work, 6–12 keys per acre is a healthy low-rise density, and luxury villa resorts often drop to 4–8 keys per acre. A master plan tests both views and settles on a room count that is both legal and sellable.

Stage 5: Phasing and Future Growth

Few owners build a whole hotel in one go, and a good master plan does not ask them to. It shows a first phase that can open and earn on its own, and reserves land for what comes next.

  • Phase one usually covers the arrival, the main public areas, a core of rooms and the service spine, which is the hardest part to add later.
  • Later phases add rooms, villas, a larger banquet, a spa or more parking as occupancy and cash flow grow.
  • Reserved land is kept clear of anything that would block expansion, such as a water tank or a substation in the wrong place.

At Mukteshwar, the master plan combines 45 private villas, serviced studio residences and a resort clubhouse. A shared hospitality core of this kind can be built first and serve every later phase.

Approvals That Shape the Master Plan

Several approvals change what the master plan can contain, so we map them during site analysis instead of leaving them until the drawings are done. Requirements vary by state, and everything here must be checked locally:

  • Change of land use (CLU). Agricultural or residential land usually has to be converted to commercial or hotel use first.
  • Building plan approval and fire NOC. The local authority sanctions the drawings, and the fire department approves exits and fire systems under the National Building Code.
  • Environmental clearance. Needed once the built-up area crosses the threshold, generally 20,000 sq m, which a large hotel reaches quickly.
  • Hill, river and eco-sensitive rules. Sites in the hills or near rivers, forests and sanctuaries face extra limits on height, slope cutting and density.
  • State tourism registration. In many states this also opens up hotel incentives or single-window clearance.

For a fuller view of costs and approvals on hospitality projects, see our resort planning and cost guide.

Hospitality Consultant or Master Planning Architect: Who Does What?

Owners often ask whether to hire a hospitality consultant or an architect first. On most hotel projects you need both, and their work overlaps at the master plan.

Hospitality consultant Master planning architect
Market study and financial feasibility Site analysis and what the land can carry
Recommends the hotel positioning, room count and facilities Turns that into a space programme and a site layout
Operator and brand selection Checks the plan against bye-laws, approvals and brand standards
Pre-opening and operations planning Phasing, building design and construction drawings

Large consultancies such as Horwath HTL list services from site selection and facilities programming to the project master plan. For a smaller hotel or resort, an architect experienced in hospitality can often prepare the programme and master plan directly with the owner, and bring in a consultant for the financial study. Our guide to the hotel feasibility study explains how the two fit together.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is included in hotel master planning services?

A hotel master plan usually covers site analysis, a space programme listing every part of the hotel and its size, the room count and mix, zoning of the site into arrival, public, rooms, leisure and service areas, separate guest and service routes, a check against building rules and approvals, and a phasing plan for future growth. Detailed building and interior design follow afterwards.

How many rooms can I build on my land?

It depends on two things. The local rules set the maximum through FAR (or FSI), ground coverage, height limits, setbacks and parking. The type of hotel sets the target: a city hotel builds close to the maximum, while a resort-style hotel spreads out, typically at 6–12 keys per acre for low-rise resorts. A master plan tests both to reach a room count that is legal and sellable.

What is the difference between a hotel master plan and hotel architecture?

The master plan decides what goes on the land, how big each part is, how people move and what is built in each phase. Hotel architecture then designs the buildings themselves: floor plans, elevations, rooms and interiors. The master plan comes first because its decisions are the most expensive to change later.

Do I need a hospitality consultant or an architect first?

On larger projects, both work together early. The consultant studies the market and finances and recommends the room count and facilities, and the architect tests that brief against the site and the rules and turns it into a master plan. For a smaller hotel or resort, many owners start with an experienced hospitality architect and bring in a consultant for the feasibility study.

How do FAR and ground coverage affect hotel design?

FAR sets the total floor area you can build, and ground coverage sets how much of the plot the building can occupy at ground level. Together with height limits and setbacks, they decide whether the hotel is a compact tower or a low, spread-out building, and so they shape the room count, parking and open space. Both vary by city and zone, so confirm them for your plot before finalising a plan.

Plan the Land Before the Building

A hotel's success is decided early: by how well the land is read, how honest the programme is, how cleanly guests and service are separated, and whether the plan leaves room to grow. Those decisions cost little on paper and a great deal once they are built.
