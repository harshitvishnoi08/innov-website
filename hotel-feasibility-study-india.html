Hotel Feasibility Study in India
How the Numbers and the Architecture Fit Together

Ar. Udit Vishnoi 19 November 2026 12 min read

Before a hotel is designed, someone has to answer a blunt question: will it make money on this site, at this size, at this cost? A feasibility study answers it. The architecture answers a second question that is just as important: can the hotel the numbers need actually be built here?

Most guides on hotel feasibility in India are written by financial consultants, and they treat the building as a single cost line. In our experience as hospitality architects, that is where many studies go wrong. The cost per room, the share of space given to banquets, the efficiency of the floor plates and the phasing all come from design decisions. If those decisions are guesses, so is the financial model.

This guide explains what a hotel feasibility study contains, how the market and financial work connects to architectural planning, which numbers decide whether a project goes ahead, and the mistakes we see most often. It follows on from our guide to hotel master planning in India, which covers the site and layout side in depth.

What a Hotel Feasibility Study Answers

A feasibility study tests whether a proposed hotel is viable before serious money is committed. In plain terms, it answers four questions:

  • Is there demand? Who would stay here, why, in which months, and at what room rate?
  • What should be built? The category, the number of rooms, and the restaurants, banquet, spa and other facilities the market will pay for.
  • What will it cost? Land, construction, interiors, equipment, fees, approvals and pre-opening costs.
  • Does it pay back? Projected revenue, operating costs, profit and return over time, under good and bad scenarios.

HVS, one of the main hotel consultancies in India, describes the feasibility study as the first step of any hotel development plan and notes that its cost is often less than 0.01% of the project sum. Compared with the cost of building the wrong hotel, that is very little.

The Five Parts of a Hotel Feasibility Study

Part What it covers Who usually leads it
Market study Demand from business, leisure, wedding and event guests, plus competing hotels and achievable room rates and occupancy Hospitality consultant
Site and technical study Access, visibility, slope, utilities, building rules, and what the land can carry Architect
Concept and area statement Room count and mix, facility list, built-up area, a first block plan and phasing Architect, with the consultant or operator
Financial model Project cost, revenue by department, operating costs, cash flow, return and sensitivity tests Hospitality consultant or financial advisor
Legal and approvals review Land use, change of land use, building approvals, fire NOC, environmental clearance and licences Architect and legal advisor

On a large branded hotel, a consultancy usually leads and the architect supports the site and concept work. On a smaller hotel, a boutique property or a resort, the owner often starts with an experienced hospitality architect and brings in a consultant for the market and financial study. Either way, the five parts have to agree with each other.

Where Architecture Comes Into the Numbers

The financial model needs a project cost, and the project cost depends on how much building the hotel needs and how expensive that building is. Both are architectural questions.

The area statement

An area statement lists every space in the hotel and its size: rooms, corridors, lobby, restaurants, banquet halls, kitchens, back-of-house, parking and services. It turns a room count into a total built-up area. An area statement that is too generous inflates the cost. One that is too tight produces a hotel that cannot operate properly. We build it from the space programme described in our master planning guide, then check it against the site and the building rules. Our back-of-house planning guide explains what the service areas need to hold.

Cost per key

Hotel costs are compared per key, meaning per room, including that room's share of the public areas and services. The figures our studio uses as starting points for the Indian market, excluding land, are:

Hotel positioning Cost per key (excl. land) Built-up cost
Mid-scale / 3-star ₹35–60 lakh ₹3,000–4,500 / sq ft
Upscale / 5-star ₹1–1.8 crore ₹6,000–9,000 / sq ft
Luxury / boutique ₹1.5–3.5 crore ₹9,000–15,000+ / sq ft

Design choices move a project up or down within these bands: basements, facade, long-span banquet halls, finish level, terrain, and whether rooms are in one block or spread across villas. A feasibility study that uses a single generic figure misses all of this.

Efficiency and phasing

Two hotels with the same room count can have very different built-up areas, depending on how efficiently corridors, cores and public spaces are planned. A more efficient plan cuts cost per key without cutting what the guest experiences. Phasing also changes the numbers: opening with a core of rooms and public areas, and adding rooms or a banquet later, reduces the money needed up front and lets the hotel start earning sooner.

The Numbers That Decide Go or No-Go

A few figures carry most of the weight in any hotel study. Here is what each one means.

  • Occupancy. The share of available rooms sold over a period.
  • ADR or ARR (average daily or room rate). The average price paid per occupied room.
  • RevPAR (revenue per available room). Occupancy multiplied by ADR. It shows how much each room earns on average, whether sold or not.
  • Revenue mix. How much comes from rooms compared with food and beverage, banquets, spa and other sources.
  • Project cost and return. Total cost, operating profit, payback period and return on investment under different scenarios.

What the Indian market looks like

For a national benchmark, the HVS Anarock India Hospitality Industry Overview 2025 reports, for calendar year 2025:

Measure (India, 2025) Reported figure
Nationwide occupancy 63–65%
Average room rate About ₹8,500–8,700
RevPAR About ₹5,400–5,600
New brand signings About 64,118 keys across 586 properties
New openings About 14,199 rooms across 176 properties

These are national averages across many hotel types and cities. Your own study needs figures for your micro-market and category, which can be very different.

A simple way to read the figures

As an illustration only: at the national average RevPAR of about ₹5,500, one room earns roughly ₹20 lakh a year in room revenue (₹5,500 × 365 days), before operating costs. Compare that with a mid-scale cost of ₹35–60 lakh per key, or ₹1–1.8 crore per key for a 5-star, and it shows how hard room revenue has to work to repay an upscale hotel, and why food, beverage and banquets matter so much in India. A real study works this out properly, with operating costs, financing and a ramp-up period in the first years.

Reading the Market in India Today

A feasibility study is a snapshot of a market, and the Indian market is shifting. The HVS Anarock overview notes that growth is increasingly coming from Tier 2, 3 and 4 cities, helped by better connectivity, rising local demand and lower development costs. Branded hotels are moving into markets they ignored a decade ago.

For an owner with land in a smaller city or on a highway, that is an opportunity, but it raises the bar for the study. Demand in these markets is often driven by weddings, local business, pilgrimage or weekend travel rather than corporate stays. A good study looks at those sources specifically, and the architecture follows: a hotel in a wedding-led market needs a banquet hall, lawns, kitchens and parking sized for big events, which can matter as much as the rooms. Mistakes That Make a Feasibility Study Misleading

HVS lists the usual causes of loss-making hotels as ignoring local demand and supply, getting the project cost wrong and missing timelines, which together lead to overspending and over-building. From the design side, we would add a few more:

  • A cost per key copied from elsewhere. Using a figure from another city, another category or another year, without checking it against this site and this design.
  • No area statement. Estimating cost from a room count alone, without listing the lobby, banquet, kitchens, back-of-house and parking that make up a large part of the building.
  • Ignoring the site. A sloping, narrow or flood-prone plot, or a tight height limit, can add cost or cut room count in ways the model never saw.
  • Treating banquets as an extra. In much of India, banquets and weddings are a major revenue line and need serious space, parking and kitchen capacity.
  • No phasing. Modelling only the full hotel, when a phased opening could bring the project within budget.
  • Brand standards added late. Bringing in an operator after the design is fixed, then redesigning to meet its room sizes and back-of-house rules.

How the Study Changes for Different Hotel Types

The same five parts apply to every hotel, but the questions that matter most change with the kind of property.

Hotel type Demand to test Design questions that move the numbers
City business hotel Corporate stays on weekdays, meetings, and what fills the hotel at weekends How many floors the rules allow, basement parking, and how compact the floor plates can be
Highway or banquet-led hotel Weddings and local events, transit travellers, and the length of the wedding season Banquet hall and lawn size, kitchen capacity for several functions, and event-day parking
Resort Weekend and holiday travel from nearby cities, weddings, and how strong the off-season is Villas or a room block, spread of the site, landscape and pool, and staff housing
Boutique or heritage hotel Guests paying for character and experience, often at higher rates but in smaller numbers Conservation work, fitting services into an old structure, and a small room count carrying the costs

For a resort, our resort planning guide covers density and cost per key in more detail. For heritage buildings, the cost of conservation and of fitting modern services into old walls has to be in the study from the start, because it rarely shows up in standard cost ranges. What to Have Ready Before You Commission a Study

A study moves faster and costs less when the owner brings the basics to the first meeting:

  • Land documents. Title, revenue records, a survey or at least a boundary sketch, and the land use shown in the master plan.
  • Access details. The width of the road the land faces, and any second road for a service entry.
  • Your idea of the hotel. The category, rough room count and facilities you have in mind, even if they change later.
  • Budget and funding. How much you can invest, how much will be borrowed, and whether you plan to build in phases.
  • Brand intentions. Whether you want a brand to run the hotel, or plan to operate it yourself.
  • Local knowledge. Hotels nearby you see as competitors, and the events or seasons that bring visitors to the area.

None of this needs to be perfect. Even a rough version lets the architect and the consultant focus on the questions that matter for your site, instead of starting from nothing.

The Right Order: Who Does What, When

  1. Idea and site. The owner has land or an opportunity and a rough idea of the hotel.
  2. Site check and best use. An architect checks what the land can carry and whether a hotel is the right use at all. Our guide to planning a large land parcel covers this step.
  3. Market and concept. The consultant studies demand and recommends the positioning, while the architect turns it into a room mix, an area statement and a first block plan.
  4. Financial model. The consultant or advisor models costs and revenue, using the architect's areas and cost ranges.
  5. Operator and brand. If the hotel will be branded, the operator is chosen and its standards are built into the concept.
  6. Go or no-go. The owner decides, and only then does detailed design and the approvals process begin.

The architect does not need to be fully appointed for the design at this stage, but should be involved early enough that the areas, costs and site constraints in the study are real.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is included in a hotel feasibility study?

A hotel feasibility study usually has five parts. A market study covers demand, competition, room rates and occupancy. A site and technical review checks what the land can carry. A concept sets the room count, facilities and area statement. A financial model works out costs, revenue, operating profit and return. A legal review covers land use, approvals and licences. Together they show whether the hotel is viable and in what form.

Why does a hotel feasibility study need an architect?

The financial model depends on the project cost, and the project cost depends on how much building the hotel needs and what kind of building it is. An architect checks what the site can carry, prepares the area statement and gives realistic cost-per-key ranges for the design. Without that, the study relies on generic figures that may not fit the site.

What is the average hotel occupancy and room rate in India?

The HVS Anarock India Hospitality Industry Overview 2025 reports nationwide occupancy of about 63–65% for calendar year 2025, with an average room rate of about ₹8,500–8,700 and RevPAR of about ₹5,400–5,600. These are national averages, and individual cities and hotel categories can be very different.

How much does it cost per room to build a hotel in India?

Excluding land, a mid-scale (3-star) hotel or resort typically costs about ₹35–60 lakh per key, an upscale 5-star about ₹1–1.8 crore per key, and a luxury or boutique property about ₹1.5–3.5 crore per key. Location, terrain, basements, finish level and the share of space given to banquets and spa move the figure within these ranges.

When should a hotel feasibility study be done?

Before the land is bought if possible, and always before detailed design starts. At that stage the study can still change the size, category, phasing or even the decision to build a hotel at all. Once design is fixed, a feasibility study can only check the numbers, not improve the project.

Test the Hotel Before You Build It

When the market work, the site, the area statement and the cost figures are developed together, the study tells an owner something real: what to build, how big, in what order, and whether it will pay.
