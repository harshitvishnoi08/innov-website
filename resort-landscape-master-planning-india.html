Landscape Master Planning for Resorts in India
Planning the Land Before the Buildings

Ar. Udit Vishnoi 21 November 2026 12 min read

Guests remember a resort by its landscape as much as its buildings: the walk from arrival to the room, the lawn at sunset, the view across the water, the shade of an old tree. In a good resort, that landscape is planned first, and the buildings are placed within it.

Too many resorts in India are planned the other way round. The buildings are drawn, the roads and parking are fitted around them, and whatever land is left is handed to a gardener. The result is a resort with gardens but no landscape: no structure, no sense of arrival, poor drainage and a water bill that never stops growing.

This guide explains how landscape master planning for resorts works in India, step by step: reading the land, using landscape as the structure of the plan, water, planting, outdoor spaces, regional approaches, cost and maintenance. It draws on resorts and hospitality sites our studio has planned, from a riverfront resort on the Ganga at Prayagraj to a hill resort community on a Himalayan ridge in Mukteshwar.

What Landscape Master Planning Means for a Resort

Landscape master planning is not the same as garden design. It happens at the start of a project, alongside the architectural master plan, and decides how the whole site works as one place.

Landscape master plan Garden or planting design
Done at the start, with the architecture Usually done once buildings are fixed
Decides where buildings, roads, water and open space go Decides what goes in the spaces left over
Plans drainage, water storage and the arrival sequence Plans flower beds, lawns and specimen trees
Protects existing trees, slopes and views Works with what remains after construction
Considers maintenance, water use and phasing over decades Often focused on the opening-day look

Hospitality firm WATG describes this as a land-first philosophy: understanding the site deeply and bringing landscape thinking into every phase from the start, which it links to lower operating costs through reduced water and energy use, better biodiversity and stronger guest connection to the place.

Step 1: Read the Land

Every landscape master plan begins with a site study. Before deciding where anything goes, we map:

  • Topography. Slopes, high points, low points and level areas. High ground is often best for views, and gentle slopes for buildings and lawns.
  • Water. Where rain flows, collects and drains away, plus any river, stream, lake or groundwater. Water decides more about a resort landscape than almost anything else. For sites on a river, lake or coast, see our guide to waterfront resort architecture.
  • Existing trees and vegetation. Mature trees take decades to grow and should be surveyed and protected wherever possible.
  • Soil. What will grow, how well it drains, and whether it needs improving.
  • Climate. Sun path, prevailing breeze, rainfall and temperature range, which shape shade, planting and the placement of outdoor spaces.
  • Views and surroundings. What the site looks out at, and what needs screening, such as a road or neighbouring buildings.

At the Sangam Pooja resort in Prayagraj, the riverbank analysis came before anything else. On the 2.5-acre riverfront site, it set the master plan's orientation toward uninterrupted river views, with resort villas stepping down toward the water along the natural gradient. At Mukteshwar, a slope analysis of the ridge set where every villa could sit along the contour.

Step 2: Make the Landscape the Structure of the Plan

Once the land is understood, the landscape becomes the frame that holds the resort together. We plan the open spaces first and place buildings within them, which is the reverse of fitting gardens between buildings.

A landscape spine

Most good resort plans have a central landscape element that everything relates to: a lake, a lawn, a river edge, a garden axis or a ridge. In our concept master plan for a 15-acre villa community in New Chandigarh, a curving landscaped lake forms the spine of the whole site, with a resort-style clubhouse and villas arranged around it. At Sangam Pooja, the river itself plays that role, and river-facing banquet lawns open onto it.

The arrival sequence

The journey from the gate to reception is the guest's first experience of the resort. A planned sequence of tree-lined drive, a glimpse of the view, a shaded drop-off and a framed first look across the landscape sets the mood before anyone checks in. Parking should sit off this route, screened by planting.

Zones and buffers

Landscape also separates the parts of a resort that should not meet: quiet rooms from event lawns, guest paths from service yards, the resort from the road. Planted buffers, level changes and water can do this far more gracefully than walls.

Step 3: Plan the Water

In India, water is both a resort's greatest attraction and its greatest risk. Too much of it in the monsoon floods lawns and paths. Too little in summer turns lawns brown and drives up costs. The landscape master plan has to manage both.

  • Drainage first. Follow the natural flow of water across the site, keep buildings out of drainage lines, and give rain a place to go through swales, channels and soak pits.
  • Harvest and store rainwater. Roofs, paved areas and lawns can feed storage tanks, recharge pits or a pond that holds monsoon water for the dry months.
  • Water as a feature. Ponds, lakes, channels and pools can double as storage, cooling and the main attraction. A recent Free Press Journal report on a resort in Odisha describes how designers studied local hydrology and built a 10-acre lake with a one-acre peninsula for the resort buildings.
  • Water for cooling. Water bodies near outdoor spaces cool the air around them. At The Bliss in Bijnor, water bodies integrated near the stage help microclimatic cooling during large gatherings.
  • Reuse. Treated wastewater from the resort's sewage treatment plant can irrigate lawns and planting, which cuts fresh water demand.

Step 4: Choose Planting That Belongs

Planting decides how the resort looks, how comfortable it feels and how much it costs to run.

  • Native and climate-suited species. Plants that suit the local soil and rainfall need less water, fewer chemicals and less replacement, and they attract local birds and insects.
  • Keep the old trees. A mature tree gives shade, character and a sense of permanence that no new planting can match for decades. Plan buildings and paths around them.
  • Shade where people walk and sit. In most of India, shade is the most valuable thing a landscape provides. Tree-lined paths, pergolas and planted courtyards make the resort usable in the heat.
  • Plant to screen. Service yards, plant rooms, parking and boundary walls disappear behind well-planned planting. At The Bliss, planting is positioned to buffer harsh southern sunlight and reduce heat gain.
  • Lawns where they earn their water. Lawns are thirsty. Use them where guests actually gather, such as event lawns and play areas, and use groundcovers, meadows or gravel gardens elsewhere.

Step 5: Design the Outdoor Rooms

A resort's outdoor spaces are rooms in their own right, and many earn money directly.

Outdoor space What to plan for
Event and wedding lawns Size for the largest event, level ground, drainage, power points, a stage position, and access for caterers that avoids guest areas
Pool and pool deck Sun and shade through the day, the view, privacy from rooms, and a deck that can host small functions
Dining terraces The best views, shelter from wind and rain, and a short route to the kitchen
Wellness gardens Quiet, shade, natural materials and distance from event noise
Paths and trails Comfortable gradients, shade, clear wayfinding and lighting for the evening

At Namah Stay in Agra, a long event lawn takes large seated receptions while guest villas wrap a separate central pool deck, so events and stays share the site without disturbing each other. Our guide to destination wedding resort architecture covers event landscapes in detail.

Lighting the landscape

Many resort evenings happen outdoors. Warm, low-level path lighting, uplit trees and softly lit water make the landscape safe, beautiful and photogenic after dark, without flooding it with light or disturbing wildlife.

Landscape Approaches Across India

India's climates call for very different landscapes. A plan that works in Kerala will fail in Rajasthan. The same is true of the buildings, as our guide to climate-responsive hotel design in India shows.

Setting Landscape priorities
North Indian plains Deep shade, water for cooling, monsoon drainage, and lawns kept to where they are used
Hills and mountains Following the contour, protecting slopes and forest, managing runoff, and framing views
Riverfronts Flood levels, river setbacks, stable banks, and orienting spaces to the water
Coast and backwaters Salt and wind-tolerant planting, high water tables and a fragile ecosystem
Hot and dry regions Courtyards, water conservation, drought-tolerant planting and stone-paved shade

In Kerala, for example, the master plan for Adira Resort drew its layout from the site's existing canals and set out to respond to the delicate backwater ecosystem. For hill sites, our guide to mountain hotel architecture covers slopes and drainage in depth.

What a Landscape Master Plan Includes

When the landscape master plan is complete, an owner should have a clear set of drawings and decisions that the architects, engineers and contractors can all build from:

  • Site analysis maps of slopes, drainage, trees, soil, sun, wind and views.
  • A landscape framework plan showing the open-space structure, the landscape spine, the arrival sequence and the buffers between zones.
  • A grading and drainage strategy setting levels, where water flows, and how it is collected, stored and released.
  • A tree survey and retention plan marking which trees stay, which are moved, and where new trees go.
  • A planting strategy with the palette of species for each zone, chosen for climate, water use and character.
  • Outdoor space layouts for lawns, pool decks, terraces, gardens and paths, with sizes and uses.
  • A water budget estimating irrigation demand and how much can come from harvested and treated water.
  • A lighting concept for paths, trees, water and outdoor spaces.
  • A maintenance outline describing the care the landscape will need, and roughly what it will cost to run.

Phasing the Landscape

Most resorts open in phases, and the landscape should be phased with them. A few principles help:

  • Plant the big trees first. Trees take years to mature, so the main avenue and shade trees should go in as early as possible, even on land that will be built on later.
  • Build the water system early. Drainage, storage ponds and the treatment plant are hard to add later and protect everything built afterwards.
  • Finish what guests see in phase one. The arrival, the first rooms, the pool and the main lawn should look complete on opening day, even if other parts of the site are still to come.
  • Hold future land lightly. Areas reserved for later phases can be meadow, orchard or nursery in the meantime. A nursery on site also grows plants for later phases at a fraction of the cost.

Cost and Maintenance

Landscape takes a real share of a resort budget. In our resort planning and cost guide, landscape, water and wellness facilities together are often 10–20% of the build. The final figure depends on how much earthwork, water infrastructure, hardscape and mature planting the plan needs.

For design fees, a landscape cost guide by Studio Matrx gives common ranges in India of about ₹15–80 per sq ft, or 8–15% of the landscape build cost. The same guide warns that upkeep over five years can exceed the original build cost, a point that matters even more on a resort with acres of grounds.

That is why maintenance should be planned with the design. Native planting, efficient drip irrigation, reuse of treated water, fewer thirsty lawns and durable paving all reduce running costs for the life of the resort.

Who Should Be Involved, and When

Landscape master planning works best when the architect and landscape architect start together, before the building positions are fixed. The civil and water engineers join early too, to plan drainage, storage, treatment and irrigation. If the landscape team arrives after the buildings and roads are set, all they can do is decorate what is left.

At our studio, landscape is part of the master planning from the first site study, so that the land, the water and the buildings are planned as one.

Mistakes We See in Resort Landscapes

  • Clearing the site first. Cutting old trees and levelling the land before the plan is made.
  • Building in drainage lines. Placing buildings or lawns where monsoon water naturally flows.
  • Lawns everywhere. Large lawns no one uses, which drink water and need constant mowing.
  • Exotic planting that struggles. Plants chosen for a photo that cannot cope with the local summer or monsoon.
  • Parking in the view. Car parks and service yards placed where guests look out.
  • No shade. Beautiful open spaces that are unusable for most of the day.
  • No maintenance plan. A landscape that looks perfect on opening day and declines from there.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is landscape master planning for a resort?

It is the planning of the whole resort site as one landscape, done at the start alongside the architecture. It decides where buildings, roads, water, open space and outdoor facilities go, how drainage and water storage work, which trees and slopes are protected, and how the landscape will be maintained over the years. Planting design comes later and works within it.

When should a landscape architect be involved in a resort project?

From the first site study, before building positions are fixed. That is when the landscape can still shape the plan: protecting trees and views, keeping buildings out of drainage lines and setting the arrival sequence. Brought in late, a landscape architect can only plant what is left.

How much does resort landscaping cost in India?

As a guide, landscape, water and wellness facilities are often 10–20% of a resort's build cost. Landscape design fees in India commonly run about ₹15–80 per sq ft or 8–15% of the landscape build cost. Maintenance should be budgeted from the start, because upkeep over several years can exceed the original build cost.

How can a resort landscape save water?

By following the site's natural drainage, harvesting and storing rainwater, reusing treated wastewater for irrigation, using drip irrigation, choosing native and climate-suited plants, and limiting lawns to the areas guests actually use.

Should a resort keep existing trees?

Wherever possible, yes. Mature trees provide shade, character and habitat that new planting cannot match for decades. A tree survey at the start lets buildings, roads and paths be planned around the best trees.

Plan the Landscape First

The best resorts feel as if the buildings were placed into a landscape that was always there. That happens when the land is studied first, water and trees are treated as assets, outdoor spaces are planned as carefully as rooms, and maintenance is designed in from the start.
