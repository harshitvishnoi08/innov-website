Corporate Campus Architecture in India
Planning Modern Workplaces

Ar. Udit Vishnoi 24 November 2026 12 min read

Since hybrid work arrived, many people come to the office only when the trip feels worth it. A corporate campus now has to earn that commute.

A corporate campus is a group of buildings, landscape and facilities designed for one organisation. In India, campuses range from company headquarters and training centres to the large technology and global capability centre (GCC) campuses that have reshaped cities like Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Chennai and Pune. The same principles apply to smaller campuses for manufacturers, research organisations and growing Indian firms in the NCR.

This guide explains corporate campus architecture and planning for Indian projects: the brief, the master plan, the rules, buildings, workplace and amenities, mobility, sustainability, security, phasing and what a campus planning consultant does. For interiors, see our guide to office interior design in Noida.

What Makes a Corporate Campus

A campus differs from an office building in three ways. It has several buildings or a large single building on its own land. It includes shared facilities such as dining, sports, training and health services. And it is planned as one place, with landscape, routes and a consistent character tying it together.

  • Headquarters campus. A company's main base, often with a strong brand presence and visitor facilities.
  • Technology and GCC campus. Large office floors for thousands of employees, with heavy IT, power and security needs.
  • Training campus. Classrooms, auditoriums and accommodation for trainees.
  • Mixed industrial and office campus. Manufacturing, R&D and offices on one site.

Why Campus Demand Is Growing in India

Global companies are expanding their Indian operations fast. According to Colliers India's report on GCCs, as reported by IBEF in September 2025, global capability centres were projected to make up 40 per cent of India's total office space market in 2025, leasing about 28 million sq ft that year. GCCs have absorbed nearly 100 million sq ft across the top seven cities since 2021, and Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Chennai together account for more than 60 per cent of that demand.

CBRE's report on GCCs found that GCC office leasing reached 29.2 million sq ft in 2024, up 29 per cent on the year before, and noted that global corporates increasingly prefer newer, green-certified integrated tech parks for their Indian set-ups. That preference shapes how new campuses are designed.

Building the Brief

A campus brief has to look ten years ahead.

  • Headcount and growth. Current staff, expected growth and the share who will be on site on a typical day under hybrid work.
  • Functions. Workspace, labs, training, data rooms, dining, sports, health, visitor and event spaces.
  • Security levels. Which areas are public, which are for staff and which are restricted.
  • Brand and culture. How the campus should feel to staff, clients and recruits.
  • Sustainability targets. Green building ratings, energy and water goals and corporate commitments.
  • Phasing. What must open first and what can follow as the organisation grows.

Choosing the Site

Many campus problems start with the site. Before buying or leasing land, test it against a few questions:

  • Talent access. Can staff reach it from where they live, by metro, bus or a reasonable drive? A cheap site that adds an hour to everyone's commute can cost more in attrition than it saves in land.
  • Land use and road width. Is office or IT use permitted, and is the access road wide enough for the FAR you need? Under the UP regulations, maximum office FAR depends directly on road width.
  • Utilities. Reliable power, water and data connections, or the space and permission to build your own.
  • Height limits. Airport funnel zones and protected monuments can cap building height.
  • Shape and size. Enough frontage for a proper arrival, and enough depth to separate the public front from service areas.
  • Room to grow. Space, or an option on adjoining land, for future phases.

Planning the Campus

Good campuses share a clear structure. We plan most in four zones:

  • The front. Arrival, visitor reception, the main lobby and any public-facing spaces such as an auditorium or experience centre.
  • The work zone. Office buildings, labs and training blocks, linked by covered walkways.
  • The social heart. Dining, cafés, a gym, sports facilities and outdoor spaces at the centre, within a short walk of every building.
  • The service edge. Utilities, loading, waste, power plant and data infrastructure at the back of the site, with their own access.

A central green or spine ties the campus together and gives it identity. Keep walking distances short, since people will not walk ten minutes in the Indian summer to reach a meeting. Shade every main route with trees, canopies or covered walkways. Reserve land for future buildings and plan roads and utilities for the full build-out from the start. The Rules: Office and IT Buildings

Rules vary by city. The Uttar Pradesh Building Regulations, 2026, covering NOIDA, Greater Noida, YEIDA and other industrial development authority areas, show the kind of provisions that shape a campus:

ProvisionOffice buildings, including IT/ITES (UP regulations, 2026)
Access roadMinimum 12 m
Base FAR2.5
Maximum FAR with purchasable FAR5.0 on 12–24 m roads, 8.75 on 24–45 m roads, unrestricted on wider roads
Green building bonus5 per cent additional FAR for GRIHA 4-star, IGBC Gold or LEED Gold, 7 per cent for GRIHA 5-star, IGBC Platinum or LEED Platinum
AtriumsSkylit atriums are not counted in FAR, with kiosks limited to 20 per cent of the atrium
Distance between blocks6 to 16 m depending on building height
HeightNo general limit, but governed by protected monuments, airport funnel zones and other statutory limits
Parking2.0 equivalent car spaces per 100 sq m of proposed FAR in metros, 1.5 elsewhere

Two provisions are worth noting. The green building bonus is awarded on pre-certification, and the regulations set a penalty of twice the land cost for the extra FAR if the committed rating is not achieved at occupancy. And on commercial plots of 2 to 4 hectares, the Authority may permit up to 40 per cent of the availed FAR for residential use, subject to an infrastructure plan, which can allow staff housing or serviced apartments on campus.

Designing the Buildings

  • Floor plate depth. Plates that are too deep leave people far from windows. Courtyards and atriums bring daylight into larger plates.
  • Flexible floors. Regular column grids, generous floor-to-floor heights and service zones that allow layouts to change as teams grow and shrink.
  • Efficient cores. Lifts, stairs, toilets and risers grouped so floors can be split for different teams or tenants later.
  • Mid-rise or tower. Mid-rise buildings around green courts suit large suburban sites. Towers suit dense urban plots and transit locations.
  • Connected buildings. Covered walkways or bridges between blocks, so people can move around the campus in rain and heat.
  • Facades for the climate. Shaded glass, fins and screens instead of fully glazed boxes. Our guide to climate-responsive design covers the principles, which apply to offices as much as hotels.

Workplace and Amenities

For many staff, amenities are now part of the reason to come in. The most valued ones in Indian campuses include:

  • Food. A large cafeteria or food court with several counters, plus cafés spread across the campus. Our guide to F&B planning covers kitchens and food courts.
  • Wellness. A gym, sports courts, a walking or jogging track and a medical room.
  • Family support. A crèche close to the entrance.
  • Learning. Training rooms and an auditorium for town halls and events.
  • Quiet and prayer rooms. Spaces for rest, reflection and prayer.
  • Outdoor work. Shaded terraces and courtyards with power and Wi-Fi.

Inside the buildings, the workplace itself has shifted towards more meeting rooms, collaboration areas and focus booths, and fewer fixed desks. Interiors and architecture should be planned together so ceilings, services and furniture layouts support this.

Workplace Strategy After Hybrid Work

Hybrid work has changed what the office is for. People come in to meet, collaborate, learn and socialise, and do focused work wherever suits them. A campus should plan for that pattern:

  • Plan for the busy days. Attendance peaks mid-week. Size meeting rooms, dining and parking for those days, and let quieter floors close on others to save energy.
  • Neighbourhoods, not rows. Teams get a home zone with a mix of desks, meeting rooms and lounge space.
  • More rooms for video calls. Small enclosed rooms with good acoustics and lighting for one or two people.
  • Event-ready space. Town halls, training and client events are now a main reason to come in, so plan flexible halls and pre-function space.
  • Hospitality thinking. Reception, cafés and lounges that feel closer to a good hotel than a traditional office lobby.

Mobility and Arrival

Most Indian campuses move thousands of people at the start and end of each shift. Arrival needs as much planning as the buildings.

  • Staff buses and cabs. Dedicated bays for company buses and app cabs, with covered waiting areas, away from the main visitor entrance.
  • Two-wheelers. Large, shaded two-wheeler parking close to building entrances.
  • Cars and EVs. Car parking to the local rules, with EV charging planned in from the start.
  • Walking and cycling. Safe routes from the nearest metro or bus stop, and shaded paths across the campus.
  • Security gates. Enough lanes and queueing space so security checks do not back traffic onto the main road.

Many Indian campuses run several shifts, including night shifts for teams serving other time zones. Plan lighting, security and transport for arrivals and departures at every hour, with well-lit, staffed drop-off points and safe routes from the gate to each building.

Sustainability

Green certification is now expected by many global tenants, and in Uttar Pradesh it also earns extra FAR. Campuses have the space to go further than single buildings:

  • Solar on roofs, parking canopies and walkways.
  • Water harvesting, treatment and reuse for flushing, cooling towers and landscape.
  • Central plant for cooling and power, sized for the whole campus.
  • Landscape with native trees that cool the site and need little water. Our guide to landscape master planning explains the approach.
  • Waste segregation and composting, especially for large food operations.

Security and Resilience

  • Layered access. A public front, staff areas and restricted zones such as data rooms, labs and executive floors.
  • Visitor management. A reception that handles visitors, vendors and interview candidates without crowding the staff entrance.
  • Power and data. Backup power, redundant data connections and secure server rooms placed above flood level.
  • Fire safety. Exits, refuge areas and fire systems designed to the state's fire services rules. In Uttar Pradesh, that means the Fire and Emergency Services Act, 2022 and Rules, 2024, which the 2026 regulations refer to.

Technology and Building Systems

  • Building management. A central system that monitors cooling, lighting, power and water across every building.
  • Data infrastructure. Redundant fibre routes into the campus and a protected backbone between buildings.
  • Smart access. Card or phone-based access that also records occupancy, so space can be planned around real use.
  • Service tunnels or trenches. On large campuses, a shared route for pipes and cables makes later changes much easier.

Phasing the Campus

Most campuses are built in phases. The first phase should feel complete: arrival, at least one office building, dining, some sports and a finished landscape around them. Later phases add buildings around the same structure. Infrastructure such as roads, drainage, power and water should be designed for the full campus, even if built in stages, so later phases do not mean digging up what is already finished.

Construction next to an occupied campus needs its own plan. Keep construction traffic on a separate gate and route, fence and screen the work site, and schedule noisy work outside peak hours. Where the organisation needs space before the permanent buildings are ready, temporary or leased space nearby can bridge the gap without compromising the master plan.

What a Campus Planning Consultant Does

  1. Brief and space programme. Turning headcount, growth and work patterns into areas and building types.
  2. Site analysis and master plan. Zoning, access, landscape, phasing and infrastructure for the full build-out.
  3. Regulatory strategy. FAR, height, parking, green bonuses and approvals.
  4. Architecture and interiors. Buildings, workplace and amenity spaces designed as one system.
  5. Coordination. Bringing together structural, services, IT, security and landscape consultants.

Common Corporate Campus Mistakes

  • Designing for today's headcount. No room to grow, or no flexibility to shrink.
  • Buildings far apart with no shade. Long, hot walks between blocks.
  • Arrival jams. Too few bus bays and security lanes for shift changes.
  • Glass boxes. Fully glazed facades that overheat and need heavy cooling.
  • Amenities as an afterthought. Dining and sports squeezed into leftover space.
  • Green ratings promised, not delivered. In UP, missing a committed rating can mean a penalty on the bonus FAR.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a corporate campus?

A corporate campus is a group of buildings, landscape and shared facilities designed for one organisation on its own site. It usually includes office buildings, dining, sports, training and health facilities, planned as one connected place with a consistent character.

Why are global companies building campuses in India?

Global capability centres are expanding fast. Colliers India projected that GCCs would make up 40 percent of India's office space market in 2025, and CBRE found GCC leasing reached 29.2 million sq ft in 2024, with growing preference for green-certified integrated tech parks.

What FAR is allowed for office buildings in Noida?

Under the UP Building Regulations, 2026 for industrial development authority areas such as NOIDA and Greater Noida, office buildings including IT offices have a base FAR of 2.5, rising with purchasable FAR to 5.0 on 12 to 24 metre roads, 8.75 on 24 to 45 metre roads and unrestricted on wider roads.

Do green buildings get extra FAR in Uttar Pradesh?

Yes. The UP Building Regulations, 2026 give 5 percent additional FAR for GRIHA 4-star, IGBC Gold or LEED Gold rated buildings and 7 percent for GRIHA 5-star, IGBC Platinum or LEED Platinum, on pre-certification, with a penalty if the committed rating is not achieved.

What does a corporate campus planning consultant do?

A campus planning consultant turns headcount and growth plans into a space programme, prepares the master plan and phasing, works through FAR, parking and approvals, designs the buildings and interiors as one system and coordinates the engineering, IT, security and landscape teams.

Design a Campus People Choose to Visit

A corporate campus works when people want to be there: when arrival is easy, buildings are comfortable, amenities are close and the landscape makes the walk between meetings pleasant. Those qualities come from the master plan, long before the first building is designed.
