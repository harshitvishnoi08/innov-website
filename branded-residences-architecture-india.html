Branded Residences in India
The Architecture Behind Luxury Living

Ar. Udit Vishnoi 24 November 2026 12 min read

A branded residence is a home sold with a hotel or lifestyle brand's name, design standards and services attached. Buyers pay more for that promise, and the architecture has to deliver it every day for decades.

Branded residences are growing fast in India, from city towers in Mumbai and Delhi-NCR to villas in Goa and the hills of Uttarakhand. For a developer, the brand can lift prices and speed up sales. It also brings fees, strict design standards and an operating model that must be planned into the building from the first sketch.

This guide explains branded residence architecture for Indian projects: what branded residences are, what the market looks like, how to choose the model, and how the brand shapes planning, units, amenities, operations and approvals. It draws on our work on resorts, villa communities and townships, including our concept for a hill resort community with serviced residences in Mukteshwar.

What Is a Branded Residence?

A branded residence is a home, usually an apartment or villa, that carries the name of a hotel group or lifestyle brand and is managed to that brand's standards. Owners get the look, the services and often the amenities of a luxury hotel, in a home they own. For rental-only managed units, see our guide to serviced apartments.

There are a few common models:

  • Hotel-integrated residences. Homes in the same building or on the same site as a branded hotel, sharing its services and amenities.
  • Standalone branded residences. Homes carrying a hotel brand but with no hotel on site. The brand manages the building and its services.
  • Resort residences and villas. Second homes in a resort, often with the option to put the home into a rental programme when the owner is away.
  • Non-hotel lifestyle brands. Fashion, design, car or wellness brands lending their name and design language to residential projects.

What separates a branded residence from a luxury apartment with a smart name is the operating agreement. A real branded residence has a contract under which the brand sets design standards, approves the product and provides or supervises services after handover.

The Branded Residence Market in India

India is now one of the larger branded residence markets in the world. According to Knight Frank's Residence Report, as reported by IBEF in October 2025, India ranks sixth globally for live branded residence projects, with about 4 per cent of global supply. It ranks tenth for the pipeline of future projects among the 83 countries surveyed. Mumbai, Delhi-NCR, Bengaluru and Pune lead, while Goa and Uttarakhand are gaining ground as lifestyle and second-home destinations.

Buyers pay for the brand. Savills' Global Brand Premium Study finds that branded residences command a global average premium of 33 per cent over comparable non-branded homes, with resort locations achieving the highest premium at 39 per cent and established and emerging cities averaging 30 per cent.

The premium is not free. WATG's Branded Residence Design Guide notes that developers typically pay the brand 2.5 to 5.0 per cent of gross sales revenue, and also carry higher design and construction costs to meet brand standards. The project has to earn enough extra to cover both.

Choosing the Right Model

The first big decision is whether the residences sit alongside a hotel or stand on their own.

ModelAdvantagesChallenges
Hotel-integratedFull hotel services and amenities, a strong brand presence, shared operating costsDeveloper must fund and build a hotel, complex shared spaces, tension between hotel guests and owners
Standalone brandedNo hotel to fund or run, simpler building, privacy for ownersServices and amenities paid for by owners alone, smaller operating team
Resort residences with rentalOwners earn rental income, the resort keeps occupancy upWear and tear on units, rental and private use must coexist

Knight Frank's research on how to build a luxury branded residence notes that many developers do not want to fund or operate a hotel, and that standalone residences also avoid competing with hotel inventory. Location matters as much as the model. WATG calls setting the single biggest factor in branded residence design, because it shapes density, layout and how owners use their homes.

Setting the Brief With the Brand

A branded residence has three clients: the developer, the brand and the future owners. The brief has to satisfy all three before design starts.

  • Brand standards early. Each brand has its own rules for arrival, unit sizes, finishes, amenities and back-of-house. Fixing them at concept stage avoids expensive redesign later.
  • Unit mix. Knight Frank advises against oversized apartments, because fewer units means fewer owners sharing the running costs. Build in flexibility, since buyer profiles can shift during a four- to five-year development.
  • Service level. Decide which services will be offered, such as concierge, housekeeping, in-home dining, valet and maintenance. Each one needs space, staff and a route.
  • Rental or private. Decide whether owners can rent their units through a programme, because that changes access, finishes and operations.

Planning the Building and Site

Arrival and access

The arrival has to feel like a five-star hotel, with a covered drop-off, a lobby with a concierge and a sense of privacy. In hotel-integrated projects, owners and hotel guests should not share the same front door. WATG's guide is clear that privately occupied residences need separate entrances, circulation and often their own amenities, while rental units integrate closely with hotel servicing. Our guide to hotel lobby and public area design covers the arrival sequence in more detail.

Circulation and service cores

Branded residences run on services, so the building needs a service lift and back-of-house routes that reach every unit without crossing owner corridors. Housekeeping trolleys, in-home dining, deliveries and maintenance should use their own route. This is the same logic as a hotel, explained in our guide to hotel back-of-house and circulation planning.

Parking and security

Indian luxury buyers often own several cars and employ drivers. Plan enough parking, a waiting area for drivers, valet access and secure entry points. Keep security discreet. Controlled lifts and a staffed lobby do the job without visible barriers.

Amenity floors and zones

Group amenities so they are easy to staff and easy to reach. A podium or clubhouse that holds the gym, spa, pool, lounge and dining can be run by one team.

Designing the Residences

Owners test the brand promise inside their own homes every day. A few principles we follow:

  • Hotel-grade durability for rental units. WATG notes that rental units see far more footfall than ordinary homes, so specifications should lean towards hotel-grade durability.
  • Customisation within limits. Owners want to personalise their homes. Offer a choice of finishes and layouts within the brand's standards.
  • Kitchens that are easy to service. WATG identifies kitchens as a servicing challenge because they take time to clean. Durable surfaces and simple details help the housekeeping team.
  • Indian living patterns. Many Indian luxury homes need a room for domestic staff, a utility area, generous storage and sometimes a separate prayer space.
  • Lock-and-leave features. Second-home owners want to close the door and leave. Secure storage, smart controls and remote monitoring make that possible.
  • Views and outdoor space. Balconies, terraces and gardens matter even more in a home than a hotel room, because owners spend longer there.

Interiors and Brand Identity

Interiors carry most of the brand's identity. The lobby, the corridors, the amenity spaces and the show apartment are where buyers first feel what they are paying for. A few things matter here:

  • One design language. Architecture and interiors should be designed together so the building, the public spaces and the homes feel like one place. Gaps between an architect and a separate interior designer show up in awkward ceilings, misplaced services and finishes that do not meet.
  • Brand codes, local character. International brands have signature details, but buyers in India respond to craft, materials and references they recognise. Regional stone, carved timber or textiles can sit within the brand's standards. Our guide to vernacular architecture in hospitality explains how.
  • Lighting and acoustics. Hotel-quality lighting scenes and sound insulation between homes are part of what owners expect from a brand.
  • Finishes that last. Public areas take heavy use, so choose stone, timber and fabrics that can be cleaned and refreshed in place.

Amenities That Build Community

Amenities were once a checklist of pool, gym and clubhouse. Knight Frank finds that buyers now look for community and a shared way of living, with wellness, hospitality and curated social spaces becoming central to value. In practice that means:

  • Wellness. Spa, fitness, yoga and pool spaces designed to the brand's hotel standard.
  • Social spaces. A residents' lounge, private dining room and terraces where owners actually meet.
  • Family spaces. Play areas, a children's room and garden space for Indian families who often live with several generations.
  • Work spaces. Quiet rooms and meeting spaces for owners who work from home.

Every amenity has a running cost. Knight Frank points out that staffing is the largest contributor to service charges, so amenities should be planned to be run efficiently from day one.

Designing for Operations and Long-Term Value

A branded residence is judged for decades after the launch. Owners pay service charges and expect the brand to keep its promise. Design decisions affect that directly.

  • Refurbishment cycles. Knight Frank notes that maintenance and refurbishment typically run on a five- to seven-year cycle. Choose materials that can be refreshed without major works.
  • Efficient staffing. Compact back-of-house, central stores and logical service routes reduce the number of staff needed.
  • Climate and corrosion. Coastal and humid locations wear materials quickly. Specify accordingly. Our guide to waterfront resort architecture covers coastal materials.
  • Energy and water. Efficient systems reduce service charges and support the brand's sustainability commitments.

Phasing, Sales and the Show Experience

Branded residences are usually sold off-plan, so the design has to sell before the building exists.

  • A sales gallery and show apartment. Buyers want to touch the finishes and walk through a real room. Build a full-size show unit that matches the final specification exactly.
  • Visuals that tell the truth. Renders and walkthroughs should show what will actually be built, including the real view and orientation.
  • Phased delivery. On larger sites, deliver the clubhouse and key amenities with the first phase of homes, so early owners get the promised lifestyle from day one.
  • Flexible later phases. Keep later phases open to changes in unit mix as the market responds.

For phasing on large sites, our guide to large land parcel planning covers how infrastructure and amenities are sequenced.

Resort and Second-Home Branded Residences

Resort locations earn the highest premiums in the Savills study, and in India the growth is visible in Goa, the hills and destination markets. Resort residences work differently from city towers. Owners visit for weeks at a time, often put their homes into a rental programme and expect the resort to look after the property when they are away.

Our concept for a hill resort community in Mukteshwar follows this logic. On an 11-acre Himalayan ridge, the plan combines 45 private villas, a cluster of serviced studio apartments positioned as staffed, lock-and-leave secondary homes, and a resort that provides the shared hospitality core. The resort clubhouse sits at the highest point of the site, and the villas follow the contour. The concept shows how a resort can carry the services that make second homes easy to own.

For hill sites, see our guide to mountain hotel architecture. For broader resort planning, see how to design a resort in India.

Approvals and Regulation in India

A branded residence is a real estate project first, so it follows the same approvals as any housing project, plus extra steps where a hotel is involved.

  • Land use. Residential, commercial and hotel uses may carry different FAR, height and parking rules. Mixed sites may need land use permission for each part.
  • RERA registration. Residential projects sold to buyers generally need registration with the state real estate regulator, with disclosures on the project, amenities and timelines.
  • Hotel approvals. Where a hotel is part of the project, it needs its own fire, health and tourism approvals and, if desired, star classification.
  • Hill, coastal and eco-sensitive rules. Second-home markets often sit in regulated landscapes, with limits on height, density and distance from water.
  • Larger sites. Villa communities on large parcels may fall under township or group housing rules. See our guide to the township approval process in India.

How We Approach Branded Residence Projects

Branded residences sit where hotels and homes meet, and that is the ground our studio works on. Our hospitality work covers resorts, banquet venues and hotel interiors. Our residential work covers luxury villas, neo-classical homes and villa townships. A branded residence draws on both.

  1. Test the site and market. Unit mix, density and amenities checked against the location and the likely buyer.
  2. Work to the brand's standards. Arrival, units, amenities and back-of-house planned to the brand's technical requirements from the first concept.
  3. Design architecture and interiors together. One studio for the building and the homes, so the brand experience is consistent.
  4. Plan for operations. Service routes, staffing and refurbishment considered alongside the look.

Common Branded Residence Mistakes

  • A brand on a generic building. Buyers pay a premium for quality and service. A logo alone will not hold the price.
  • Brand standards too late. Redesigning the arrival, units or back-of-house after the brand signs is expensive.
  • Shared entrances. Owners and hotel guests crossing in one lobby leaves both unhappy.
  • Oversized units. Fewer, larger homes push service charges up for every owner.
  • Amenities nobody staffs. A spa or lounge that is too expensive to run ends up closed.
  • Ignoring Indian living patterns. No room for domestic staff, too little storage or poor parking for drivers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a branded residence?

A branded residence is a home, usually an apartment or villa, sold with the name of a hotel or lifestyle brand and managed to that brand's standards. Owners get hotel-style services and amenities, and the brand approves the design and supervises operations under a contract with the developer.

How big is the branded residence market in India?

Knight Frank's Residence Report, reported in October 2025, ranks India sixth in the world for live branded residence projects, with about 4 percent of global supply. Mumbai, Delhi-NCR, Bengaluru and Pune lead, and Goa and Uttarakhand are emerging second-home destinations.

How much more do branded residences sell for?

Savills' Global Brand Premium Study puts the global average premium at 33 percent over comparable non-branded homes, with resort locations highest at 39 percent. Premiums vary widely by city, brand and product quality.

How much does a brand charge the developer?

WATG's Branded Residence Design Guide says developers typically pay the brand 2.5 to 5.0 percent of gross sales revenue. Developers also carry higher design and construction costs to meet brand standards, so the premium has to cover both.

Do branded residences need separate entrances from the hotel?

In hotel-integrated projects, privately occupied residences generally need their own entrance, circulation and often their own amenities, while units in a rental programme connect more closely to hotel services. Separate access protects owner privacy and keeps hotel operations running smoothly.

Design the Promise Into the Building

A brand can sell a branded residence once. The building has to keep selling it for decades through its arrival, its homes, its amenities and the services that run quietly behind them. That only happens when the brand, the operations and the Indian way of living are planned together from the start.
